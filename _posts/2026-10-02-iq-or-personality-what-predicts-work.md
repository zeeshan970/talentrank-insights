If you've taken both an IQ-style test and a personality test, you've probably wondered which result matters more for your career. The research has a fairly clear answer, and it's not the one most people expect: neither score wins by a mile, and the gap between them has shrunk a lot in the last few years.

![IQ or personality: what predicts work, two scores answering two different questions]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/iq-or-personality-work-hero.webp)

## What the old numbers said

For about two decades, the go-to source was a 1998 meta-analysis by Frank Schmidt and John Hunter. It pooled decades of hiring studies and put general mental ability (roughly, what IQ tests try to capture) at the top of the list for predicting job performance. Its estimated validity was .51, which in hiring research is very high. Conscientiousness, the Big Five trait about being organized and dependable, came in well below that.

That paper shaped hiring advice for years. If you wanted one number about a candidate, the advice went, get their cognitive ability score.

## What changed in 2022

In 2022, Paul Sackett and colleagues went back over the same kind of data and argued that the older studies over-corrected for a statistical problem called range restriction. Once they adjusted the method, most of the numbers dropped. Cognitive ability fell to about .31. Structured interviews came out on top, at about .42.

Conscientiousness also came out lower than cognitive ability, but the two were no longer in different leagues. The practical reading is simple: thinking ability still matters, it just isn't the near-guarantee it was once sold as.

## Why the two scores measure different things

An IQ score is mostly about how quickly you learn and reason your way through problems you haven't seen before. That helps most in jobs that keep throwing unfamiliar problems at you, and early in a job, when you're still learning it.

Personality scores describe habits. Someone high in conscientiousness plans ahead and follows through without being chased. That matters every single week, including in year five of a job, long after the learning curve has flattened.

So the two answer different questions:

- **IQ:** can you figure this work out?
- **Personality:** will you keep doing it well, and in a way that suits you?

## A hypothetical example

Say two analysts join the same team. One scores higher on reasoning tests and picks up the tools in half the time. The other scores higher on conscientiousness, keeps clean notes and never misses a deadline.

In month one, the first analyst looks stronger. By month twelve, the picture is often mixed: the fast learner may be bored, and the steady one has built a reputation the manager relies on. Neither result was "wrong". They were measuring different parts of the same job.

## What an IQ score can't tell you

A single number hides a lot. It doesn't show which kinds of thinking you're strongest in, and psychologists still argue about how many [types of intelligence](https://www.talentrank.io/blog/types-of-intelligence) are worth separating. It also says nothing about motivation, interests or how you handle stress.

If you want to know what the number does and doesn't cover, TalentRank's guide on [what IQ actually measures](https://www.talentrank.io/blog/what-does-iq-measure-an-honest-guide-to-cognitive-ability-iq-scores-and-what-they-actually-predi) is a fair, plain-language place to start.

## How to use both results

If you have both scores, read them together rather than ranking them.

1. Use your reasoning score to judge how much new learning a role will ask of you, and whether you'd enjoy that pace.
2. Use your personality profile to judge the day-to-day fit: structure versus freedom, people versus focus work, pressure versus predictability.
3. Look hardest at conscientiousness if you're weighing a role with little supervision. TalentRank's page on [what a conscientiousness score means](https://www.talentrank.io/blog/conscientiousness-what-your-score-means-for-success) explains the high, middle and low ranges.
4. Treat both as starting points for a conversation with yourself, not as a ceiling.

## The short answer

IQ predicts a bit more of job performance on average, especially for complex and new work. Personality explains a different and more lasting part of it. The 2022 re-analysis makes the gap smaller than the textbooks used to say, which is good news if your scores don't line up the way you hoped.
